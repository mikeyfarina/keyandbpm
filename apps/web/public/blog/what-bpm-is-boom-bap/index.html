What BPM is boom bap?

By Mike Farina · 29 September 2026

Boom bap is usually made between 85 and 95 BPM, with most of it falling somewhere from 80 to 100. The kick-and-snare pattern puts the snare on beats 2 and 4, so what you nod to is the real tempo, though BPM tools sometimes report double (170 to 190) because of busy, swung hi-hats.

What BPM is boom bap?

90 is the number people reach for, and it's a good one. Most boom bap sits between 85 and 95 BPM. Go down to 80 and it starts to drag in a way some people love; push past 100 and it starts sounding like something else. That's the convention, not a law, and there are great records outside it.

The name is the drum pattern said out loud: "boom" is the kick, "bap" is the snare. Kick on the one, snare on two and four, with the kick pattern moving around in between. It's the sound of 1990s New York hip hop, built from drum breaks sampled off funk and soul records.

Is boom bap half-time or double-time?

Neither, and that's what makes it different from trap or drill. In a boom bap beat the snare hits on beats 2 and 4, so the tempo you nod to and the tempo the DAW shows are the same number. Nod along to a 90 BPM boom bap beat and you'll nod 90 times a minute.

Compare that with trap, where the session says 140 but the single clap per bar makes it feel like 70. Boom bap at 90 and trap at 140 are closer in feel than the numbers suggest: 90 in full time versus 70 in half time.

Detectors still get it wrong sometimes. Here's why:

  • Busy hats. If the hi-hats play steady eighths, a beat tracker can decide those are the beats and read 180 instead of 90.
  • Swing. Swung eighths are unevenly spaced, which blurs the pulse and makes the detector less confident.
  • Sampled breaks. A break lifted off a 1970s record was played by a person, and it may drift a little. Loop it and the drift repeats every bar.

If you get a reading in the 170s or 180s for a boom bap track, halve it. If you get something in the 40s, double it. The details, and why tools pick the wrong multiple at all, are in why your BPM reads half or double.

Why does boom bap swing?

Swing is the bigger half of boom bap. Two beats at 90 BPM can feel nothing alike if one is dead straight and the other has its sixteenths pushed late.

Swing means delaying every second note in a pair. In a straight groove, sixteenth notes are evenly spaced. With swing, the second sixteenth of each pair arrives late, so the hats and ghost notes lope instead of tick. On Akai MPCs, swing is a percentage: 50% is straight, and the numbers above it push the offbeat later. Producers each have their favourite setting. I'd start somewhere in the mid-50s and move up until it feels right, then back off a touch.

The machines matter to the history here. The E-mu SP-1200 (1987) and the Akai MPC60 (1988) were the tools a lot of classic boom bap came out of. The SP-1200 had only about ten seconds of sampling time in total, at 12-bit, which is why producers sampled records at a faster speed and pitched them back down, and why so much of the era sounds gritty. The MPC's swing and its pads shaped how people programmed drums.

Then there's J Dilla, who took swing somewhere else. Instead of a single swing setting, his drums were often played in by hand with quantise turned off, so the kick, snare and hats each drift slightly against each other. That "drunk" feel is a big influence on lo-fi hip hop, and it's also the hardest thing in hip hop to fake with a swing knob.

What goes into a boom bap beat?

A sampled break or a hard-hitting kit

Classic boom bap drums come from breaks: the bit of a funk or soul record where the band drops out and the drummer plays alone. Producers chopped those into single hits and re-sequenced them, or looped a bar. The kick is punchy rather than long and subby. The snare cracks.

A sample on top

Usually a loop or chops from jazz, soul, funk or library records. A lot of the craft of boom bap is here: finding the right four bars, cutting them cleanly, and filtering the bass out so the kick has room. If you're new to the chopping side, how to flip a sample covers cutting and re-sequencing.

A bassline that follows the sample

Often a filtered bass from the sample itself, sometimes a separate bass played on top. It has to be in the same key as the sample, which brings up tuning.

Why are my boom bap samples out of tune?

Because the records they came from often weren't tuned to A440 in the first place. A lot of older records were recorded or mastered at a slightly different speed, and speed changes pitch. Then the producer who sampled it may have sped it up or slowed it down again. By the time a loop lands in your session, it might be 30 cents sharp of any standard key.

That matters when you add a bass or keys on top. If you play a bassline in A minor over a sample that's actually in A minor but 30 cents sharp, it'll sound sour even though the notes are "right." The reasons old records drift are in why old records aren't tuned to 440 Hz.

The analyser on this site handles this in two ways. It measures the key against the record's own tuning, so a detuned soul loop still gets the right key name. And it tells you the tuning itself, as the frequency of A4 in Hz and as cents away from 440, so you know how far to detune your synth or sampler to match.

How do I find the BPM of a boom bap loop?

Tapping works well on boom bap, better than on most genres, because the snare on 2 and 4 is exactly where your hand wants to land. Tap the snare and kick together for 15 or 20 seconds and you'll be close.

For a loop you've cut yourself, the maths is often easier than tapping. If a one-bar loop in 4/4 is exactly 2.667 seconds long, the tempo is 60 × 4 ÷ 2.667, which is about 90 BPM. Most DAWs will do this for you if you tell them how many bars the loop is.

A detector is quickest when you have a whole track. Just remember the swing and the double-time reading, and check the beat grid by pressing play. If the grid marks land on every hat instead of every kick and snare, halve the number.

What BPM should I make my boom bap beat?

Let the sample decide. If you find a loop you love and it sits at 87, make the beat at 87. Stretching a sample three or four BPM to hit a round number rarely improves it, and repitching it moves the key.

If you're starting from drums, try 88 to 92 for the classic head-nod, around 80 to 85 for something heavier and more laid back, and 95 to 100 when you want a rapper to ride something more urgent. Then spend the rest of your time on swing. That's where boom bap is won or lost.

Questions people ask

Is boom bap 90 BPM?

90 is the classic figure, and most boom bap sits between 85 and 95. The broader range runs from about 80 to 100.

Why does my BPM detector say 180 for a boom bap beat?

It counted the hi-hat eighths as beats instead of the kick and snare. Halve it to get the tempo you actually nod to.

What swing should I use for boom bap?

On an MPC-style swing setting, where 50% is straight, many producers work somewhere in the mid-50s to low 60s. There is no correct number; adjust until the hats lope without sounding sloppy.

What is the difference between boom bap and lo-fi hip hop?

Lo-fi hip hop usually runs slower, often 70 to 85 BPM, with softer drums, jazzier chords and more deliberate tape and vinyl texture. Boom bap drums hit harder and are built for rapping over.

Does boom bap have to use samples?

No, but the style grew out of sampling, and most of its sound comes from breaks and loops. Plenty of producers now play the parts in and process them to sound sampled.
